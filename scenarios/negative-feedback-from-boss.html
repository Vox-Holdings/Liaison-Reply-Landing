Taking feedback

How to respond to negative feedback from your boss steady, curious, and specific.

Short answer: thank them, ask one or two questions to understand exactly what they want to change, and say what you'll do next. Hold off on defending yourself in the moment. If you disagree, raise it calmly later with facts.

01 Copy and send

Replies to negative feedback from a manager

The best reply to criticism from your boss shows you heard it and you're on it. These templates do that in a few lines, so the real conversation can happen in person. Pick one and edit the [brackets].

Acknowledge and commit

Most situations.

Thanks for telling me, [Name]. You're right that I should have been ready for [the budget questions]. I'll [prepare a backup sheet] for the next one. Looking forward to talking it through on [day].

Ask for specifics

When the feedback is vague.

Thanks for the feedback. I want to get this right. Could you share one or two specific moments where it fell short? That will help me focus.

Respectfully add context

When there is context they may not know.

Thanks, [Name], I hear you. One bit of context for our chat: [I received the figures an hour before]. That said, I should have flagged it, and I'll make sure we have a plan for that next time.

Disagree calmly

When you see it differently.

Thanks for being direct. I see part of it differently and I'd like to talk it through on [day]. I'll bring [notes / examples] so we can look at it together.

Following up after the conversation

Show the change.

Hi [Name], following up on our chat: for [next presentation] I've [prepared a Q&A sheet and checked numbers with finance]. Let me know if anything else would help.

03 Read the message first

How to take criticism from your boss without getting defensive

Lauren's message is direct and a little sharp. She names one clear problem, the budget questions, and asks to talk. You may have context, but the message isn't asking for it yet. It's asking whether you heard.

She wants to know you get it. Show her that first.

When feedback arrives by text, keep the reply brief and open. Save context and disagreement for the conversation, where tone is easier to read.
  • “Felt underprepared.”A judgment. Ask what prepared would look like.
  • “Didn't have answers on the budget.”The specific issue. Commit to fixing it.
  • “Let's discuss in our one-on-one.”The real conversation is later. Keep this reply short.

Who you’re replying to

Lauren, my manager. She messaged that yesterday's client presentation felt underprepared because I couldn't answer budget questions. Finance sent the numbers late, but I could have flagged it. I want to acknowledge it, understand what she expects and not sound defensive.

This context shapes all three replies.

Direction Get clarity

Safe reply

Thanks for flagging it, Lauren. You're right, I should have been ready for the budget questions. For our one-on-one, could you share what you'd want covered in prep for these meetings?

Why this works: it owns the specific miss and asks for her standard, without excuses.

Warm reply

I appreciate you telling me. I was frustrated with myself in that moment too. I'd like to get this right. In our one-on-one, could we go over what you'd expect me to have ready? I'll also bring a short plan for the budget side.

Why this works: it shows you care, invites her guidance, and arrives with a plan.

Direct reply

Understood. The budget numbers arrived an hour before the meeting, and I should have flagged that to you. What would you like me to do differently on the next one?

Why this works: it gives the context once, takes responsibility for the flag, and asks a concrete question.

Not sure which to send? Safe is right most of the time. Direct is fine if your manager values context. Warm fits a supportive relationship.

Follow ups after negative feedback

What to say when the conversation continues.

  • “This isn't the first time.”

    I hear that. Could we look at the pattern together, so I know what to focus on?

    Understood. What would better look like on the next presentation?

    I should have. Next time I'll message you as soon as a piece is late.

  • “Let's put a plan in place.”

    Sounds good. I'll draft one and send it to you before Friday.

06 FAQ

Negative feedback from your boss, answered

How do you respond to negative feedback from your boss?

Thank them, ask a question or two to understand what they want, and say what you'll do next. Save context or disagreement for a calm conversation.

How do you respond to criticism at work without being defensive?

Pause before replying, acknowledge the specific point, and ask what good would look like. Avoid “but” and blame.

Should you reply to negative feedback by text or wait?

A short acknowledgment by text is good. Keep the detailed discussion for a meeting where tone is easier to read.

How do you disagree with feedback from your manager?

Thank them, say you see part of it differently, and ask to talk it through with examples. Calm facts work better than emotion.

What do you say when your boss says you're underprepared?

Acknowledge the specific gap, commit to a fix, and ask what they'd expect you to have ready next time.

How do you follow up after negative feedback?

Send a brief note showing what you've changed, such as a new checklist or prep step, and ask if anything else would help.

What if the feedback feels unfair?

Take a day, gather facts, then ask for a conversation. Focus on specific examples rather than how it made you feel.
