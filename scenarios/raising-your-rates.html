Rate increase templates

How to tell a client you're raising your rates and what to say when they push back.

Short answer: email them 30 to 60 days before the change. Give the new rate, the date it starts and what stays the same, then thank them. Don't apologize or list your costs. If they push back, acknowledge it, keep the number, and offer timing or a smaller scope instead of a discount.

01 Copy and send

Rate increase email templates you can copy

A good rate increase email to clients is short and specific. The new rate sits in the first two sentences, the start date is clear, and nothing in it sounds like an apology. Swap the [brackets] for your details, then send it from your own inbox.

Standard rate increase email to clients

An established client, sent 30 to 60 days before the change.

Subject: My rates from [date] Hi [Name], A quick heads-up before we plan [next quarter]. Starting [date], my rate for [service] moves from [current rate] to [new rate]. Anything already booked or quoted before then stays at the current rate. Scope, turnaround and how we work together stay the same. Thank you for [specific project or result]. I'm looking forward to what's next, and I'm happy to talk it through if it helps with planning. [Your name]

Retainer increase email for a long-term client

A monthly retainer. Give about 60 days and line it up with a billing date.

Subject: Our retainer from [date] Hi [Name], I'm giving you [60 days'] notice of a change to our monthly retainer. From [date], it moves from [current retainer] to [new retainer] a month. Your deliverables and included hours stay exactly as they are. I'll send an updated agreement this week so your finance team has what it needs for the [month] budget. [Number] years in, I want this to keep working well for both of us. If a quick call would help before then, send me a couple of times. [Your name]

Short notice rate increase email

You left it late. Own the timing, not the price.

Subject: A rate change, and time to plan for it Hi [Name], I should have raised this sooner, so I'm building in extra time. My rate for [service] is moving from [current rate] to [new rate]. For your account, the current rate stays in place until [date at least one billing cycle away]. Nothing else changes. If it helps, I can send a short summary of this year's work that you can share internally. [Your name]

Freelance rate increase for new projects only

Project clients. The new rate applies to the next quote, never mid project.

Subject: Rates for new projects from [date] Hi [Name], Before we scope the next phase: from [date], I'm quoting new projects at [new rate], up from [current rate]. The project we're in now stays at the fee we agreed, and anything you confirm in writing before [date] is quoted at the current rate. Same process, same turnaround. Looking forward to the next one. [Your name]

Price increase text message to a client

Only for clients you already text. Follow it with the email so it is in writing.

Hi [Name], quick heads-up before I send the details by email: from [date] my rate for [service] moves to [new rate]. Anything booked before then stays at the current rate. Happy to chat if you have questions.

Rate increase email subject lines

  • My rates from [date]
  • Our retainer from [date]
  • Rates for new projects from [date]
  • A heads-up on [year] rates
  • Planning for [quarter]: an update to my rates

Plain and specific beats clever. Vague subject lines like “Important update” read as bad news before the email is even open.

02 Timing

How much notice to give clients for a price increase

Give 30 days at minimum and 60 days for retainers and long-term clients, timed to a billing date. These are common norms in freelance and small business guidance, not legal rules. If you have a contract, its terms come first.

03 When the client replies

What to say when a client pushes back on a rate increase

You sent a good email. Four minutes later, Dana wrote back: “Wait, why is it going up?” Pushback like this is usually a request for help explaining the price increase to someone else, not a no. Here is how to read it, and three calm ways to answer.

She isn’t saying no. She’s saying she was surprised.

“Can you hold the old rate?” is an opening ask, not a final answer. The strongest reply keeps the number, takes away the surprise, and gives her something to show finance.
  • “Nothing about the scope has changed.”She needs a reason she can repeat to her boss. Point to the work, not to your costs.
  • “I’ll have to take this to finance.”She is not the final decision maker. Help her make the case for you.
  • “Six weeks’ notice.”The timing stung more than the number. You can own the timing without giving up the price.

Breathe. Three calm replies to “Why is it going up?”

You add a little context and choose one direction, Set a boundary. Liaison Reply writes it three ways: Safe, Warm and Direct. Pick one, edit it until it sounds like you, and send it yourself.

Who you’re replying to

Dana, a long-term client. I told her my retainer moves from $3,200 to $3,750 on January 1. She has to justify it to her finance team. I want to keep her and keep the new rate.

This context shapes all three replies.

Direction Set a boundary

Safe reply

Totally fair to want to look at this closely, Dana. Nothing changes on the work already booked through December. I’d be glad to put together a one-page summary for your finance team showing what we shipped this year and what the new rate covers. Would a 15-minute call on Thursday help?

Why this works: it takes her surprise seriously without treating the price as open for debate. The finance summary hands her the one thing she actually needs.

Warm reply

I hear you, and I should have raised this during our September renewal. That part is on me. The new rate reflects where my work is now, and I really want to keep working together. To make the change easier, I can keep the current rate through the end of March so you have a full quarter to plan. From April it moves to $3,750.

Why this works: you own the timing, not the price, which lowers the temperature fast. The bridge has an end date, so a kind gesture never becomes the new normal.

Direct reply

Thanks for the honest reaction. The retainer moves to $3,750 a month from January, and I’m not able to hold the old rate for another year. The scope stays exactly as it is today. If the budget can’t stretch, I’m happy to suggest a smaller monthly scope that fits $3,200. Let me know which works better.

Why this works: it answers her real question with a clear no, then gives her a real choice. She is now picking between two options instead of arguing with one.

Not sure which to send? If Dana is a long-term client you want to keep close, start with Warm. If you have already been flexible with her before, Direct protects you. Safe works when you need a day to think.

Other ways clients push back, and what to say

  • “That's more than we budgeted.”

    Understood. If budget is the limit, let's adjust the scope rather than the rate. If we drop [deliverable], the monthly total stays close to what you pay now. Want me to sketch that option?

  • “Can you hold the old rate a bit longer?”

    I can keep the current rate for anything you confirm before [date]. From then, the new rate applies to all work.

  • “Other freelancers charge less.”

    That may well be true. My rate reflects the quality and turnaround you've had from me. Take a few days to think it over, and I'm glad to answer any questions.

  • “We've been loyal. I expected better.”

    I really value that, which is why I'm telling you early and directly. I want this to keep working, and the new rate is what keeps it sustainable on my side.

  • “You're too expensive.”

    I hear you. Can I ask what feels off: the total, the timing, or what's included? Then I can suggest something that fits.

What not to say in a price increase email

Most rate increase replies go wrong in the same few phrases. Here is an edit of the Warm reply with the three lines strategic coaching flags, and why each one costs you.

Live coachingCaution

Your edit of the Warm reply

I hear you, and I’m so sorry to spring this on you. I know it’s a lot, but costs have gone up for everyone lately.

Phrase notes

  • I’m so sorry to spring this on youApologizing for a price tells her it is up for negotiation.Try: I wanted to give you plenty of notice
  • I know it’s a lot, butAgreeing it is too much argues her side for her.Try: I know budgets get planned early, so
  • costs have gone up for everyone latelyA vague reason invites vague pushback, so point to the results you delivered.Try: here is what we shipped together this year

Also leave out

  • A paragraph of reasons. One line about the value of the work is enough. A list of your costs invites a debate about each one.
  • Asking permission. “Would that be okay?” turns a decision into a request.
  • Surprising them on the invoice. A new number with no warning is the fastest way to lose trust.
  • Offering the old rate before they ask. Let them respond first. Most clients accept a clear, early notice.

06 FAQ

Rate increase questions, answered

How do you tell a client you're raising your rates?

Send a short, friendly email 30 to 60 days before the change. State the new rate and the date it starts, say what stays the same, and thank them for the work together. Skip the apology and the list of costs, and offer a quick call if they want to talk it through.

How much notice should you give clients for a price increase?

30 days is the usual minimum, and about 60 days is kinder for retainers and long-term clients, ideally lined up with a billing date or their budget planning. If you have a contract, follow its notice terms first.

What should a rate increase email to clients say?

Five things: the new rate, the date it takes effect, what it applies to, what stays the same (scope and work already booked), and a line of thanks. Keep it to around 150 words so the number is easy to find.

Should you explain why you're raising your rates?

Briefly, if at all. One sentence that points to the value of your work is enough. A long justification, or blaming rising costs, sounds defensive and invites the client to argue each point.

How do you respond when a client pushes back on a price increase?

Acknowledge the reaction, keep the new rate, and offer a choice that is not a discount: more time before it applies, a smaller scope at the old budget, or a short summary they can show their finance team. Pushback is often a request for help justifying the change, not a no.

What do you say when a client says you're too expensive?

Ask what feels off: the total, the timing, or what is included. Then offer a smaller scope that fits their budget instead of lowering your rate. If the budget truly can't stretch, part on good terms and leave the door open.

Can you tell a client about a price increase by text message?

A short text works as a heads-up for clients you usually text, but follow it with an email so the new rate and start date are in writing.

Should long-term clients keep the old rate?

You can give loyal clients a longer runway, for example the current rate for one more quarter, but put an end date on it. An open-ended exception quietly becomes their permanent rate.

What subject line should a rate increase email use?

Keep it plain and specific, such as “My rates from [date]” or “Our retainer from [date]”. Vague subject lines like “Important update” read as bad news before the email is opened.
